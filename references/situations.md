# Situations Outside the Usual Flow

Short answers for the cases that don't fit buying a car from a dealer with a clean history. Route here from SKILL.md's routing table, then still apply the method: verify, don't recall, and give sources.

## Already signed

- **There's no general cooling-off period.** Once the contract is signed, the deal is final in nearly every state. "Buyer's remorse" isn't a legal right to cancel.
- **California is the exception, and its rule changed in 2026.** As of October 1, 2026, California's CARS Act (SB 766) gives buyers an automatic 3-day right to cancel most retail used-vehicle sales and leases at $50,000 or less, replacing the old optional 2-day cancellation agreement. It can carry a restocking fee and mileage limits. Verify the current fee and limits on the [DMV's page](https://www.dmv.ca.gov/) or the attorney general's site before relying on a figure.
- **Add-ons can usually still be canceled.** Extended warranties, GAP insurance and other aftermarket products are typically refundable pro rata if canceled within a window (often 30 to 60 days, sometimes longer). Check the contract's cancellation terms and the provider's rules, and cancel in writing.
- **Lemon law** covers a car with a substantial, unfixed defect, usually within a time or mileage window and after a set number of repair attempts. Rules vary by state and by new versus used. This is a case for the disclaimer: point the buyer to their state attorney general's page or a lemon-law attorney for their specific odds.

## Spot delivery and yo-yo financing

A dealer lets the buyer drive the car home before the loan is actually approved, then calls back days later to say the financing fell through or the rate is higher, and asks for more money or a new signature.

- **Don't take the car home until financing is final and in writing.** A "conditional" approval isn't an approval.
- If it already happened: the buyer isn't obligated to accept new terms. Review the paperwork for what was actually signed, and consider returning the car instead of re-signing at a worse rate. If the deal unwinds, the down payment and any trade-in go back to the buyer.

## Lease

- Compare on total cost over the term, never on payment (see `financing.md`).
- **Fees:** acquisition fee, disposition fee at turn-in, and any early-termination fee. All negotiable or at least worth asking about.
- **Residual value:** what the car is predicted to be worth at lease-end. It's set by the leasing company, not usually negotiable, and it drives the payment: a higher residual means a lower payment.
- **Money factor:** the lease's interest rate, expressed oddly. Multiply it by 2,400 to get an approximate APR for comparison.
- **Capitalized cost:** the negotiated price baked into the lease. Negotiate it like a purchase price. Don't let "it's just a lease" skip this step.
- **Skip the big down payment.** Money put down on a leased car is gone if it's totaled or stolen early, and it doesn't build equity. Negotiate the cap cost down instead.

## Private-party sale

- **Title:** confirm the seller's name matches the title, and that there's no lien, or that the lien will be paid off and released at the sale.
- **Safe payment:** cashier's check or an in-person bank transfer at the seller's bank, not cash for a large amount and not a personal check.
- **Bill of sale:** written, signed, dated, with both names, the VIN, the price and odometer reading.
- **Tax:** usually paid at the DMV when the buyer registers the car, not at the sale. Check the locale (`locale.md`) for the rate and basis.
- **Smog or emissions check:** required before registering a used car in some states. California requires a smog certificate on most private-party used-car sales before transfer.

## Trade-in only

The buyer isn't buying a car, just trying to sell or trade one.

- Get binding cash offers from online buyers (Carvana, CarMax and others). Treat a valuation guide as a starting point for the conversation, and get the actual offer in writing.
- Know the loan payoff first. If the buyer owes more than the car is worth, show that negative equity to the buyer as its own line.
- See `negotiation.md`'s trade-ins section for the trade-in tax credit and how a dealer offer compares to an outside one.

## Bad credit

- **Get a pre-approval even at a high rate.** It sets a rate the dealer has to beat and keeps the buyer from financing through the dealer sight unseen.
- **Buy-here-pay-here lots** finance in-house, often at steep rates with the loan tied to the car through a GPS tracker or starter-interrupt device. They can be the only option for some buyers, but check the total cost and the repossession terms closely before signing.
- The 10% guardrail still applies (`financing.md`), and it will rule out more cars at a high rate. Say so plainly.

## EV and plug-in notes

- **Federal clean-vehicle tax credits ended for vehicles acquired after September 30, 2025.** Don't cite them, including for a car that would otherwise have qualified. Check the maker's and the IRS's current pages rather than assuming an older credit still applies.
- **A federal deduction for interest on some new-car loans exists for 2025 through 2028,** separate from the old vehicle credits. Mention that it may apply, point the buyer to irs.gov for the current rules, and never compute it yourself: this is tax advice territory (see SKILL.md's disclaimer rule).
- State and utility EV incentives vary and change often. Verify them fresh. Don't recall one from an earlier conversation or an older model year.
